Current work

Identifying RF connectors from a phone camera

A working example of the kind of problem we take on. Live, iterating, and instructive about how this category of model actually behaves in practice.

The problem

Ten classes that look almost the same.

SMA, 1.85mm, 2.4mm, 2.92mm, and 3.5mm RF coaxial connectors × male/female. Some pairs differ by 0.6mm of physical size — invisible without a reference scale. Photos vary by distance, lighting, finger occlusion, and the angle of a hex nut. The model has to run on commodity phone hardware and abstain rather than guess wrong.

Challenges we’re working through

  • Fine-pitch class confusion — when the visual difference between two classes is smaller than the camera’s effective resolving power. Sonnet-grade vision models can’t distinguish them either; only carefully-captured training data helps.
  • Distance variation — early training data was all close-up bench shots. Real users hold the phone wherever they want. Digital zoom is not a substitute for physical-distance variation in the capture set.
  • Class imbalance vs data volume — some classes had 6× more samples than others. Naive balancing (cap to smallest) cost more accuracy than the imbalance itself.
  • Calibration over coverage — the app shows “no result, try again” below a confidence threshold rather than risk a confident wrong answer. Picking the threshold and shaping the model around it is its own design problem.

What we’ve built so far

  • 2-way EfficientNetV2-S softmax ensemble exported to ONNX, runs end-to-end on phone CPU
  • A reticle-crop UX that forces training and inference to share the same scale
  • A capture protocol + Flutter app that biases new data toward whichever classes are currently confused
  • Web-based labeling tools, snapshot-based training, and a Sonnet vision audit pass for label-quality sanity checks
